\subsection{Library Migration}

The task \sys performs---replacing a third-party dependency while preserving
observable behavior---is studied directly in the library-migration literature.
Islam et al.\ construct \emph{PyMigBench}~\cite{islam2023pymigbench}, a
benchmark of real Python library migrations mined from open-source history,
and later evaluate large language models on that benchmark~\cite{islam2025llmmigration},
finding that models handle mechanical import and call-site rewrites well and
degrade on migrations that require reconciling different execution models. Their
\emph{MigrateLib}~\cite{islam2025migratelib} assembles these steps into an
end-to-end tool. He et al.~\cite{he2021ranking} address the selection problem
that \sys's \emph{LSA} faces, ranking candidate replacement libraries by
multiple metrics rather than popularity alone.

\sys differs from this line in three respects, and shares its central
difficulty. First, the migrations here are not recovered from history: no
ground-truth target is supplied to any configuration, so selecting the
replacement is part of the task rather than an input to it. Second, migration
is coupled with deliberate diversification of first-party logic, which the
migration literature does not attempt. Third, acceptance is gated on an
executable equivalence oracle rather than on the project's own test suite
alone.

The shared difficulty is the one our results make sharpest. Migration
benchmarks are validated by whether the migrated project's tests pass, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that criterion accepting clones with serious
behavioral defects at a rate near one in two on our corpus. Our subjects
dependency-inject their I/O clients---good practice, and common in the
real projects PyMigBench draws from---which makes exactly the migrated code
path unreachable by the suite that certifies it. We read this as evidence that
test-passage-based validation understates defect rates in library-migration
evaluation generally, and not only in ours.

Closest to \sys's \emph{LMA} is the line of work that synthesizes diverse
variants by program transformation. Baudry et al.~\cite{baudry2014tailored}
generate computationally diverse variants through tailored source
transformations, and Auto-SPT~\cite{hooda2025autospt} automates the discovery
of semantics-preserving transformations---the class of rewrite our \emph{LMA}
applies. Gal\'apagos~\cite{ron2024galapagos} uses LLMs to produce N-version
program variants, and is the closest in method to \sys: it likewise treats
generation as the objective and validates variants by execution. \sys differs
in coupling variant synthesis to a dependency substitution, so that divergence
arises from both a changed library and a rewritten first-party core, and in
attributing each rejected variant to the agent that produced it.

Our \emph{GSA} applies its edits through an agent--computer interface in the
sense of SWE-agent~\cite{yang2024sweagent}: the model operates on the real file
through \texttt{open}, \texttt{search}, \texttt{goto} and \texttt{edit}
operations whose effects are validated before they are committed, rather than
regenerating the module wholesale. SWE-agent shows that the design of this
interface, and not only model capability, determines how reliably an agent can
edit code. Our experience is consistent with that finding and extends it: we
observed the editing session rewrite a call site other than the one its own
analysis had identified, and moved call-site rewriting to a deterministic AST
transformation in response, reserving the model for adapter synthesis.
